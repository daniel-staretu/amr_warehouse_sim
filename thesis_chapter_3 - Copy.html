3   STATE OF THE ART

3.1   Definition and Use Cases of Autonomous Mobile Robotics

An Autonomous Mobile Robot (AMR) is a mechatronic system that can navigate independently and intelligently in an environment. This is achieved by on-board sensor systems that continuously acquire information about the surroundings, combined with logic algorithms that process this information and produce motion commands. Unlike Automated Guided Vehicles (AGVs), which follow fixed physical guides such as magnetic floor strips or optical tracks, AMRs build and maintain a model of their environment in real time and can dynamically re-route around unexpected obstacles. The result is a platform that requires substantially less infrastructure preparation than a conventional AGV and is capable of operating in partially structured or changing environments [1]. One of the first patents for an AMR was registered in 1987, under the designation of US4638445A [3], describing a mobile robot capable of perceiving its surroundings through on-board range sensors and autonomously planning a collision-free path to a target goal.

Since their early origins, AMRs have found applications across a wide range of domains. In the military sector, unmanned ground vehicles (UGVs) are deployed for reconnaissance, logistics support, and explosive ordnance disposal in environments deemed too hazardous for human personnel; the iRobot PackBot, fielded extensively during operations in Iraq and Afghanistan, is one of the most widely recognised examples. In space exploration, the NASA Mars Science Laboratory Curiosity rover and its successor, Perseverance, represent AMRs operating under the most extreme constraints of all: no human tele-intervention is possible in real time due to communication latency, forcing the platforms to perform local hazard avoidance entirely autonomously. In the agricultural sector, precision-farming AMRs autonomously apply pesticides along crop rows, perform selective fruit harvesting, and monitor soil conditions, with commercial deployments by manufacturers including John Deere and Naïo Technologies. Each of these domains has driven distinct advances in sensing, planning, and control algorithms, which cross-fertilise into the warehousing applications discussed below.

Within the warehousing and industrial sector, AMRs have become a commercially significant technology, propelled in particular by the growth of e-commerce and the associated demand for high-throughput order fulfilment [1]. The Amazon Robotics platform (formerly Kiva Systems) deploys AMRs that lift and transport entire inventory shelving units to stationary human pickers, dramatically reducing the walking distance required per order. Goods-to-person systems following a similar paradigm are now produced by multiple vendors, including AutoStore, Locus Robotics, and 6 River Systems. Beyond shelf transport, AMRs are used for autonomous pallet stacking and retrieval with forklift-style platforms, cycle counting and inventory scanning with sensor-mounted survey robots, and cross-docking logistics between receiving and shipping areas.

The rapid advancement brought forth by Industry 4.0 has created an increasing need for closer collaboration between humans and robots. Rather than operating in physically segregated cells, robots in modern production and logistics environments are expected to share workspaces with human operators. This requirement has led to the development of the collaborative robot (cobot) paradigm. Unlike traditional industrial robots, which are enclosed by safety fencing to prevent human contact, cobots are specifically designed to work in direct proximity to humans on shared tasks, using force-torque sensing, speed and separation monitoring, or power-and-force limiting to ensure safety [2]. Standards such as ISO 10218-1 [5] and ISO 10218-2 [6] specify the safety requirements for industrial robot systems, while ISO/TS 15066 [7] extends these requirements specifically to collaborative operations, defining four collaboration modes and their associated risk assessment procedures. Compliance with these standards is a prerequisite for any AMR that operates in the same space as human workers, as is the case in the warehousing scenarios considered in this project.

3.2   ADAS-Specific Object Recognition Techniques Based on LiDAR

Advanced driver-assistance systems (ADAS) represent a fusion of technologies used to aid drivers in controlling a vehicle. ADAS uses an array of sensors such as depth or 2D cameras, LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging), RADAR (Radio Detection and Ranging), and ultrasonic sensors for environment perception on roadways. Sensor data is computed locally using a combination of different algorithms, giving the vehicle a varying range of decision-making capabilities that ensure roadway safety. Some examples of ADAS features are presented in [4]. They include human and object detection and tracking, collision warning and emergency braking. Many of these features also map directly to AMR safety requirements in collaborative working scenarios.

Roadway environments are described by their unpredictable nature and a high number of edge cases. This means that even in a fully automated vehicle, a human must still be aware and ready to take control so that safety is ensured. Warehousing environments, by comparison, are best described as controlled and predictable. They have a smaller number of edge cases, which allows for full robotic autonomy. This is also relevant for the simulation of the environment used in this project.

LiDAR sensors use emissions of light to determine the shape of the surrounding environment and to measure distances. They are based on the time-of-flight principle, which states that the time taken by an emitted ray of light to reach and return to the sensor can be directly used to determine the distance. This principle takes advantage of the constant nature of the speed of light to provide accurate measurements, which are stored as individual points. When a multitude of measurements from a singular scan are put together, they form a point cloud which directly maps to the shape of the environment. This data is particularly important in obstacle detection and localisation within the environment. Techniques such as SLAM (Simultaneous Localisation and Mapping) heavily rely on LiDAR data to determine the relative position of the robot within the environment, and to aid navigation. While these sensors are highly accurate, they are unable to measure aspects related to the visual properties of objects.

Cameras represent a widely used sensor in perception systems. They work by capturing a succession of images across different colour channels. The result is a representation of the environment which is analogous to human vision. The captured data can provide rich semantic information, such as texture, colour, and shape. However, they are very susceptible to environmental conditions and can be affected by disruptions such as noise, camera glare, darkness, or dust. When compared with LiDAR, cameras also have poorer depth perception.

The techniques analysed in the following section focus on ADAS-specific object recognition methods based solely on LiDAR or on multimodal LiDAR–camera sensor fusion.

3.2.1   LiDAR Object Detection Algorithms

PointNet and PointNet++

PointNet [8] is the foundational end-to-end deep learning architecture for raw, unordered point clouds. It applies a shared Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) independently to each point, followed by a global max-pooling operation, producing a single permutation-invariant global feature vector suitable for classification or segmentation. While PointNet demonstrated that neural networks can process point clouds directly without voxelisation, its global pooling step discards local geometric relationships, limiting performance on complex multi-object scenes.

PointNet++ [9] addresses this limitation by introducing a hierarchical set abstraction mechanism. The algorithm proceeds in three stages per level. First, Farthest Point Sampling (FPS) selects a set of representative centroid points that are maximally spread across the cloud. Second, ball-query grouping collects a fixed number of neighbouring points within a radius around each centroid. Third, a mini-PointNet encodes each local neighbourhood into a single feature vector, capturing local geometric structure at the scale defined by the query radius. Multiple such levels are stacked with increasing radii, building a hierarchical feature representation from fine to coarse geometry. For dense prediction tasks such as per-point semantic segmentation, Feature Propagation (FP) layers upsample the downsampled features back to the original point count using inverse-distance weighted interpolation combined with skip connections from the corresponding set abstraction level. PointNet++ is well suited to indoor environments because its multi-scale grouping adapts naturally to the non-uniform point density that arises when objects at different distances are scanned by a rotating LiDAR.

BEV-Based Methods and PointPillars

A complementary direction to point-based methods is to project the 3-D LiDAR cloud onto a 2-D Bird's Eye View (BEV) grid and apply standard image-processing convolutional networks to the resulting raster. BirdNet [11] is an early example of this paradigm: it encodes the point cloud into a multi-channel BEV map (height statistics, point density, and intensity per cell) and applies a standard 2-D object detection CNN to produce oriented bounding boxes with heading estimates. Because the encoding and inference operate entirely in 2-D, BirdNet achieves real-time throughput with modest hardware requirements, demonstrating that mature image-based detection architectures can be adapted to LiDAR data through a projection step.

VoxelNet [10] generalised the feature-learning approach of PointNet to the full 3-D voxel space. The point cloud is partitioned into equally-sized 3-D voxels; a small PointNet called Voxel Feature Encoding (VFE) processes each non-empty voxel independently and produces a fixed-length feature vector. The resulting sparse 3-D feature volume is then processed by a 3-D convolutional middle layer and a 2-D Region Proposal Network. VoxelNet achieved strong detection accuracy on KITTI but at the cost of inference speed (approximately 4.4 Hz), making it unsuitable for real-time deployment and motivating subsequent pillar-based methods.

PointPillars [12] addresses the inference bottleneck of VoxelNet by replacing 3-D convolutions entirely with 2-D operations. The x-y plane is discretised into a grid of vertical columns called pillars. Each point within a pillar is augmented with nine features: the four raw coordinates and intensity [x, y, z, r], the cluster-relative offsets [Δx, Δy, Δz], and the pillar-centre coordinates [xp, yp]. A shared linear layer with Batch Normalisation and ReLU, followed by channel-wise max-pooling over all points in the pillar, produces a single feature vector per pillar. These vectors are scattered onto a dense (C, H, W) pseudo-image matching the BEV grid resolution and passed to a standard 2-D CNN backbone with an SSD detection head. Because no 3-D convolution is required at any stage, PointPillars achieves an inference rate of approximately 62 Hz on KITTI, roughly four times faster than prior art at comparable accuracy. This combination of speed and accuracy makes PointPillars the standard pillar-based baseline against which subsequent architectures are measured, and its pseudo-image representation forms the basis of the multimodal model used in this project.

PV-RCNN

PV-RCNN [13] introduces a two-stage detection architecture that combines the complementary strengths of voxel-based and point-based feature extraction. In the first stage, a 3-D sparse convolutional backbone processes the voxelised point cloud and generates an initial set of 3-D region proposals through a Region Proposal Network, capturing rich contextual geometry across the full detection range. In the second stage, a set of keypoints is sampled from the point cloud using farthest point sampling to provide precise localisation. Each keypoint aggregates features from nearby voxels through a voxel set abstraction module, producing a compact global representation that captures both local geometric detail and large-scale context. These pooled keypoint features are then used by a refinement head to improve the initial proposals, yielding fine-grained 3-D bounding box predictions. PV-RCNN set state-of-the-art results on the KITTI 3-D detection leaderboard at the time of publication, demonstrating that the combination of voxel-level spatial context and point-level geometric precision is highly effective for outdoor 3-D object detection. The higher computational cost of the two-stage pipeline compared to single-stage methods such as PointPillars represents the main practical trade-off.

3.2.2   LiDAR–Camera Sensor Fusion for Object Detection

LiDAR–camera fusion methods attempt to combine the complementary strengths of the two modalities: the metric depth accuracy and occlusion robustness of LiDAR with the dense semantic and texture information provided by cameras. Three broad fusion paradigms exist. Early fusion concatenates raw sensor data before any feature extraction, preserving maximum cross-modal context at the cost of tight sensor coupling. Late fusion runs independent detection pipelines per modality and combines their outputs, providing robustness to single-sensor failures at the cost of losing spatial cross-modal correspondences. Multi-stage or intermediate fusion incorporates camera information at an intermediate stage of the LiDAR pipeline, achieving a favourable balance between the two extremes.

VirConvNet

VirConvNet [14] addresses a specific limitation of approaches that generate virtual 3-D points by depth-completing a 2-D image: the resulting virtual point density is highest near the sensor (where real LiDAR coverage is already adequate) and falls off at range (where coverage is most needed). The architecture introduces two novel components to counteract this imbalance.

The first component, Stochastic Voxel Discard (StVD), randomly removes a fraction of virtual voxels during training in the near-range region where real LiDAR coverage is sufficient. This regularises the model against over-reliance on redundant near-range virtual points and forces it to learn useful representations for the sparser far-range regime.

The second component, the Noise-Resistant Sub-manifold Convolution (NRConv), encodes voxel features jointly in 3-D LiDAR space and 2-D image space. Conventional sparse convolution is applied to the 3-D voxel grid; image features sampled at the projected pixel location of each voxel are concatenated before the linear encoding step. Because depth-completed virtual points can introduce position noise when the depth estimate is inaccurate, NRConv is designed to suppress this noise by weighting the image feature contribution according to a learned confidence estimate derived from the local LiDAR density.

In this project, VirConvNet is adapted from its original 3-D bounding-box detection formulation to per-point semantic segmentation. The SSD detection head is replaced by a BEV segmentation head; per-point class logits are gathered by indexing the BEV output at each point's pillar coordinate. The ImageBackbone (a lightweight 2-D CNN at stride 4) produces a 32-channel feature map from the RGB image; real LiDAR points are augmented with features bilinearly sampled at their projected pixel positions, and virtual 3-D points are generated from image foreground regions not covered by real LiDAR returns. The virtual point generation and pillarization steps are implemented in NumPy and executed on the CPU, while the BEV backbone runs on the GPU. An auxiliary 2-D segmentation loss provides a gradient path to the ImageBackbone across the NumPy computation boundary.

3.3   Overview of Path Planning Algorithms in Robotics

Path planning in robotics is the process of generating a route for a robot to move from its starting point to its destination. This route must avoid obstacles while also following additional imposed limitations. These algorithms are used before the robot starts its motion.

Path planning usually begins with an environment modelling step, which creates a map of the area where the robot operates. This is followed by a path searching step, where a feasible path that starts from the initial point and which ends at the goal is computed. Path searching is done entirely within the model of the environment, this step also being where constraints are considered. Once the path is determined, the robot can execute its movement.

Broadly, path planning algorithms can be classified into analytical, learning-based, and hybrid methods. Analytical methods, also called classical methods, are represented by traditional rule-based path planning algorithms, and can be categorised into graph-based planning algorithms and sampling-based algorithms. Learning-based methods were developed much later and are mainly inspired by innovations found in nature, with examples including evolutionary algorithms, swarm optimisation, and neural network-based planning [16]. These methods utilise adaptive or population-based search techniques, which let the algorithm improve or learn solutions incrementally. They are appropriately used when dealing with complex or high-dimensional problems. Hybrid methods employ elements from both analytical and learning-based methods.

3.3.1   Graph-Search Algorithms

Graph-based algorithms work by dividing the environment at predetermined points into two elements: nodes, which represent possible positions for the robot, and edges, which account for all possible transitions between nodes. The most notable graph-search algorithm is A* [17], widely recognised for its efficiency in optimisation tasks in navigation and robotics, and upon which many variations have been built.

Other examples of graph-search algorithms include Dijkstra's algorithm, Breadth-First Search, and Depth-First Search [18]. They can, however, suffer in robotic applications within large environments due to their lack of a heuristic function component. In path generation, the heuristic greatly reduces the number of evaluated nodes by assigning values called weights to the transitions of the graph. It is used as a cost component and is typically chosen to be proportional to the direct distance from the node to the destination. However, it can also be chosen according to implementation-specific criteria.

A* has a heuristic component. However, the algorithm is best suited for static environments where obstacles are known beforehand, much like the other graph-based methods presented above. This computational cost represents a problem for real-time, dynamic robotic applications. For this reason, A* variations with live replanning capabilities have been introduced in algorithms such as LPA* (Lifelong Planning A*), D*, and D* lite [19].

The main advantage of D* lite over A* is its ability to reuse the parts of the paths that are not affected by obstacles when one is encountered. A comparative analysis of A* and D* lite conducted on a differential drive robot is presented in [20], where the quality of produced trajectories and the required computational time are measured and compared. According to the results, D* lite produces shorter paths on average, and is faster to compute in dynamic environments. However, A* generally outperforms D* lite in static environments.

Graph-based path planning methods find a large range of applications in differing types of robots. In [21], an example of an implementation of a D* lite variation for a coastline and harbour unmanned surface vehicle path finding is shown, while [22] implements A* in unmanned aerial vehicle navigation.

3.3.2   Sampling-Based Algorithms

The other subset of analytical algorithms are the sampling-based algorithms. These methods employ a stochastic approach to pathfinding. Unlike graph-based methods that require a complete environmental map, these algorithms build nodes and edges incrementally by randomly sampling points in the free space, creating nodes at these points, and connecting them. The Rapidly-Exploring Random Tree (RRT) and the Probabilistic Roadmap algorithm (PRM) are two examples of this approach.

The RRT algorithm [23] works by gradually building a tree that starts at the initial position. At each step, a random point in the configuration space is sampled, along with the closest node to that point. New nodes are generated by expanding in the direction of the point by a fixed step size, unless an obstacle intersection is detected. The process repeats until a node is generated within close distance to the goal node, at which point those two are connected and the full path is completed.

While RRT is suited for unmapped environments, the generated paths are often jagged and can have slow convergence to optimal results [24]. In many situations a post-processing step for path smoothing is used. RRT* [25] represents an extension of the base RRT algorithm which incorporates a rewiring step that constantly refines the structure of the tree as it is built.

The PRM [26] algorithm employs a two-phase approach: a learning phase and a query phase. In the learning phase, random configurations are sampled from the environment, which are connected through straight lines to their neighbours using a local planner, unless the generated connections collide with obstacles. This step forms a roadmap, represented through a discrete graph with nodes and transitions. The query step connects the start and goal nodes to the roadmap, after which graph search methods such as A* or Dijkstra's are applied to find the best path.

This algorithm is quite efficient in situations where the same map is reused multiple times with differing start and goal configurations. Moreover, the performance of PRM degrades in narrow passages due to the lower probability of sampling valid, non-obstacle-intersecting points [27].

3.3.3   Evolutionary Algorithms

An important learning-based pathfinding algorithm subset is represented by evolutionary algorithms. They work by using simulated evolution processes to find optimal paths. One of the most popular techniques in this category is the Genetic Algorithm (GA) [28], in which a new path is represented using a gene-containing chromosome. Genes represent the robot's intermediate positions, from the starting point to the goal. The evolutionary algorithm improves the set of potential paths through three main operations: selection, crossover, and mutation.

During the selection process, candidate paths with a better fitness score, i.e. those that are shorter, safer, or smoother, have a greater likelihood of being chosen for the production of offspring. In the crossover process, genes from two parents are recombined to produce an offspring. In the mutation process, a small amount of randomness is applied to each gene in order to ensure that the population does not converge prematurely and to retain sufficient variety.

The result of this process is that the algorithm will explore a large number of combinations of path segments simultaneously, while continuing to favour those combinations that provide better solutions. As the population continues to evolve, the paths will converge towards the optimal solution for the robot to utilise when navigating in the environment.

In [29], a GA approach is implemented and tested in a mobile robot path planning application. The environment in which the tests took place was static and represented as a grid. This paper demonstrates that GA can evaluate multiple paths simultaneously. However, a main disadvantage noted in the study is the tendency of GA towards premature convergence in highly constrained environments. Another noted disadvantage is the high computational cost, which constrains GA implementation in real-world solutions.

The GA approach can also be used for Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) planning, as shown in [30]. The paper introduces a method where candidate paths are encoded as genetic sequences and evolved to avoid local optima. Importantly, the fitness function is modified to encode the kinematic and safety constraints of UAVs. Similarly to the previous study, the high computational cost is also noted.

3.3.4   Swarm Intelligence Algorithms

Another category of learning-based methods is the Swarm Intelligence method, which is based on the behaviour of biological systems such as flocks of birds or colonies of ants. Within it, the Particle Swarm Optimisation [31] and the Ant Colony Optimisation [32] algorithms are notable examples.

In the Particle Swarm Optimisation (PSO) algorithm, a set of candidate solutions called particles explore the space of solutions. Each particle maintains a memory of the best solution it has discovered so far, called the personal best, as well as the global best solution discovered so far by the entire particle swarm. At each iteration, the velocity of each particle is updated using a weighted combination of three components: inertia, a cognitive factor, and a social factor. While the inertia factor maintains the previous velocity, the cognitive and social factors move the particle towards the best personal and global solutions respectively. This iterative process of information sharing and the balance of exploitation and exploration allows the swarm to gradually converge towards the optimal or near-optimal solutions in the search space.

The Ant Colony Optimisation (ACO) method is inspired by the paths ants take between sources of food and their nest, and the pheromone trails generated by them. Similarly to analytical methods, the environment is represented as a graph, but this method additionally uses artificial pheromones deposited by agents while moving and exploring paths. The solution is generated probabilistically using the pheromone levels left on the edges and a heuristic component. Once the agents have completed a path, pheromones are deposited proportionally to the quality of the solution, and older trails slowly evaporate over time, reducing their deposited values and encouraging exploration. The algorithm repeatedly builds solutions and updates pheromone values, ultimately converging towards the optimal or near-optimal solution.

3.3.5   Neural Network-Based Planners

A category of learning-based planning algorithms which has seen a great increase in interest in recent years comprises the Neural Network-based planners (NN) [33], inspired by the behaviour of their biological neural counterparts. They are built from interconnected nodes, called neurons, that either propagate or inhibit signals. A key advantage of NNs is their ability to learn patterns from input data, which makes them very adaptable to different situations.

Neural networks are used in many applications, such as natural language processing and image detection and generation. However, path planning implementations have specific challenges. Unlike image classification or language processing, the neural network must deal with the dynamic nature of the environment while respecting geometric and kinematic constraints. Early implementations were based on simple feedforward neural networks that mapped environment input directly to motion commands. However, these solutions were found to be deficient in terms of the ability to generalise to different environments.

Recent solutions use a fusion of different deep learning architectures such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for feature extraction and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) for decision-making. While reinforcement learning-based solutions have been found to be particularly effective in path planning, they also have drawbacks. The sim-to-real gap is one such example, where neural networks trained on virtual environments can fail to generalise to real-world implementations. Another issue is the high computational, monetary, and time costs associated with the training process. Acquiring the relevant datasets for each implementation represents another issue, as they greatly vary from implementation to implementation.

In [34], an approach combining odometry, deep learning-based artificial vision, and SLAM is presented. The system uses an NVIDIA Jetson Nano embedded system to control an autonomous mobile robot. It recognises obstacles and conveys their locations to a local navigation path planner by using two CNN architectures, ResNet18 and YOLOv3. This allows for real-time scene perception and object detection. The ResNet18-based vision system demonstrated the efficacy of deep learning algorithms in autonomous mobile robot applications, achieving an average accuracy of 98.5% across simulation and real-world trials.

3.3.6   Hybrid Pathfinding Methods

A main observation identified in the survey presented in [35] is that many state-of-the- art approaches in the literature use hybrids of analytical and learning-based methods. In general, analytical path planning methods are unreliable in dynamic environment implementations, where they can be computationally expensive and thus harder to scale. Another noted observation is that the main analytical algorithm adapted in hybrid approaches is A*, which is usually paired with either genetic algorithms or deep learning. There are many such examples of hybrid algorithms, most of which are experimental in nature. The following paragraphs will discuss two notable examples.

One example is Neural A* [36], which uses a heuristic determined by a deep learning approach that is then applied in a traditional A* algorithm for pathfinding. Neural A* can be applied to point-to-point graph searches, as well as top-down raw image inputs. It manages to generate a path faster by exploring significantly fewer nodes than A*. In spite of the improvements made by this approach, significant disadvantages also present in A* still remain, foremost being the fact that Neural A* is still mainly suited only for static environments. According to the conclusion of the paper, the presented implementation is not made to work on non-continuous spaces or those with more than two spatial dimensions.

A solution to this problem presented in [37] is Hybrid A*, which addresses the issues posed by Neural A*. While simple A* assumes a holonomic vehicle and a discrete space, the proposed model is applied in a continuous, three-dimensional space and considers vehicle orientation. Thus, the paths Hybrid A* generates satisfy real vehicle kinodynamic constraints. The nature of the terrain it traverses is also considered and used to optimise the path. Because of these factors, Hybrid A* can be more readily deployed in real-world robotic vehicle applications.
