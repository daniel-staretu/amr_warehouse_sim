3   STATE OF THE ART

3.1   Definition and Use Cases of Autonomous Mobile Robotics

An Autonomous Mobile Robot (AMR) is a mechatronic system that can navigate independently and intelligently in an environment. This is done by using logic algorithms which are given sensorial information as an input. One of the first patents for an AMR was registered in 1987, under the designation of US4638445A [3]. Ever since then, the scope of their applications has expanded greatly. For example, these types of robots have found multiple uses in domains such as the military, space technology, and agriculture. In the military domain, Unmanned Ground Vehicles (UGVs) such as the iRobot PackBot have been deployed for reconnaissance and improvised explosive device detection in operational theatres, reducing the exposure of human personnel to life-threatening hazards [a]. In space exploration, the NASA Mars Science Laboratory rover Curiosity and its successor Perseverance operate as fully autonomous science platforms, relying on on-board hazard avoidance because the communication latency with Earth makes real-time teleoperation infeasible. In the agricultural sector, AMRs are used for precision herbicide spraying, selective fruit picking, and autonomous soil condition monitoring; commercial platforms from vendors such as Naïo Technologies and John Deere have demonstrated practical viability at production scale [a].

Within the warehousing and industrial sector, AMRs have found applications such as goods-to-person shelf transport, autonomous pallet retrieval and stacking, inventory scanning, and cross-docking logistics between receiving and shipping areas. The Amazon Robotics platform, formerly Kiva Systems, pioneered the large-scale deployment of AMRs that autonomously lift and carry entire shelving units to stationary human pickers, substantially reducing per-order walking distances. Similar systems are now offered by vendors including AutoStore, Locus Robotics, and 6 River Systems, and autonomous forklift platforms capable of operating alongside human operators are increasingly deployed in distribution centres [a].

The rapid advancement brought forth by Industry 4.0 has found an ever-increasing need for closer collaboration between humans and robots. This has led to the development of the collaborative robot (cobot) paradigm. Unlike traditional robotic systems, cobots are specifically designed for working directly with other humans on tasks. This means, however, that compliance with strict safety precautions is required. Standards such as ISO 10218-1/2 [b][c] and ISO/TS 15066 [d] are used to specify the safety requirements for cobots.

3.2   ADAS-Specific Object Recognition Techniques Based on LiDAR

Advanced driver-assistance systems (ADAS) represent a fusion of technologies used to aid drivers in controlling a vehicle. ADAS uses an array of sensors such as depth or 2D cameras, LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging), RADAR (Radio Detection and Ranging) and ultrasonic sensors for environment perception on roadways. Sensor data is computed locally using a combination of different algorithms, giving the vehicle a varying range of decision-making capabilities that ensure roadway safety.

Some examples of ADAS features are presented in [4]. They include human/object detection and tracking, collision warning and emergency braking. Many of these features also map directly to AMR safety requirements in collaborative working scenarios.

Roadway environments are described by their unpredictable nature and a high amount of edge cases. This means that even in a fully automated vehicle, a human must still be aware and ready to take control, so safety is ensured. Warehousing environments, by comparison, are best described as controlled and predictable. They have a smaller amount of edge cases, which allows for full robotic autonomy. This is also relevant for the simulation of the environment.

LiDAR sensors use emissions of light to determine the shape of the surrounding environment and to measure distances. They are based on the time-of-flight principle, which states that the time taken by an emitted ray of light to reach and return to the sensor can be directly used to determine the distance. This principle takes advantage of the constant nature of the speed of light to provide accurate measurements, which are stored as individual points.

When a multitude of measurements from a singular scan are put together, they form a point cloud which directly maps to the shape of the environment. This data is particularly important in obstacle detection and localization within the environment. Techniques such as SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping) heavily rely on LiDAR data to determine the relative position of the robot within the environment, and to aid navigation. While these sensors are highly accurate, they are unable to measure aspects related to the visual properties of objects.

Cameras represent a widely used sensor in perception systems. They work by capturing a succession of images across different color channels. The result is a representation of the environment which is analogous to human vision. The captured data can provide rich semantic information, such as texture, color and shape. However, they are very susceptible to environmental conditions, and can be affected by disruptions such as noise, camera glare, darkness or dust. When compared with LiDAR, cameras also have poorer depth perception.

The technologies that will be analyzed in the following section focus on ADAS-specific object recognition techniques which are based solely on LiDAR or multimodal LiDAR-camera sensor fusion.

3.2.1   LiDAR Object Detection Algorithms

PointNet++

PointNet [e] is the foundational end-to-end architecture for raw, unordered point clouds. It applies a shared Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) independently to each point, followed by a global max-pooling operation that aggregates all per-point features into a single permutation-invariant global descriptor. While effective for object classification, the global pooling step discards all local geometric relationships, limiting its performance on complex scene-level tasks.

PointNet++ [f] addresses this limitation through a hierarchical set abstraction mechanism. At each level of the hierarchy, Farthest Point Sampling (FPS) selects a set of representative centroid points that are maximally spread across the input cloud. Ball-query grouping then collects all points within a fixed radius around each centroid, and a mini-PointNet encodes each local neighbourhood into a single feature vector, capturing geometric structure at the scale defined by the query radius. Stacking multiple such levels with increasing radii progressively builds context from fine local detail to coarse global structure. For dense per-point prediction tasks such as semantic segmentation, Feature Propagation (FP) layers upsample the downsampled features back to the original point count through inverse-distance weighted interpolation combined with skip-link concatenation from the corresponding set abstraction level. PointNet++ handles non-uniform point density through a multi-scale grouping variant that combines features from multiple radii, making it well suited to indoor environments where near objects produce dense returns and distant objects produce sparse ones.

PointPillars

BirdNet [g] was an early demonstration that projecting the 3-D LiDAR cloud onto a Bird's Eye View (BEV) grid — encoding height statistics, point density, and intensity per cell — allows standard 2-D image-detection CNNs to be applied directly to LiDAR data. While lightweight and real-time capable, BEV projection discards elevation information within each cell, motivating richer 3-D encoding approaches.

VoxelNet [h] extended the PointNet feature-learning approach to the full 3-D voxel space by partitioning the point cloud into equally-sized voxels and encoding each with a small Voxel Feature Encoding (VFE) network, followed by a 3-D convolutional middle layer and a Region Proposal Network. Although VoxelNet achieved strong detection accuracy, its 3-D convolution layers result in an inference rate of approximately 4.4 Hz on KITTI, making it unsuitable for real-time deployment and motivating the pillar-based simplification introduced by its successor.

PointPillars [i] eliminates 3-D convolution entirely by discretising the x-y plane into a grid of vertical columns called pillars. Each point within a pillar is augmented with nine features: the four raw values [x, y, z, intensity], the cluster-relative offsets [Δx, Δy, Δz], and the pillar-centre coordinates [xp, yp]. A shared linear layer with Batch Normalisation and ReLU, followed by channel-wise max-pooling over all points in the pillar, produces a fixed-length feature vector per pillar. These vectors are scattered onto a dense (C, H, W) pseudo-image and processed by a 2-D CNN backbone with an SSD detection head. By replacing 3-D convolutions with 2-D ones, PointPillars achieves an inference rate of approximately 62 Hz on the KITTI benchmark — roughly four times faster than prior art at comparable accuracy — establishing the pillar-based pseudo-image representation as the standard for real-time LiDAR detection pipelines.

PV-RCNN

PV-RCNN [j] is a two-stage detector that combines the complementary strengths of voxel-based and point-based feature extraction. In the first stage, a 3-D sparse convolutional backbone processes the voxelised point cloud across the full scene and generates an initial set of 3-D region proposals through a Region Proposal Network. In the second stage, a set of keypoints is selected from the original point cloud using farthest point sampling. A voxel set abstraction module aggregates voxel-level features from multiple radii around each keypoint, producing compact representations that combine local geometric detail with large-scale voxel context. A refinement head uses these pooled keypoint features to correct the initial proposals, yielding precise 3-D bounding box predictions. PV-RCNN set state-of-the-art results on the KITTI 3-D detection benchmark at its time of publication, demonstrating the effectiveness of point-voxel feature integration. The principal trade-off relative to single-stage methods such as PointPillars is higher computational cost, resulting in lower inference throughput.

3.2.2   LiDAR–Camera Sensor Fusion for Object Detection

LiDAR–camera fusion methods combine the metric depth accuracy of LiDAR with the dense semantic and texture information provided by cameras. Three broad paradigms exist: early fusion concatenates raw sensor data before feature extraction; late fusion runs independent detection pipelines per modality and merges their outputs; intermediate fusion incorporates camera information at an intermediate stage of the LiDAR pipeline, achieving a balance between full cross-modal context and robustness to sensor degradation.

VirConvNet

VirConvNet [k] addresses a limitation shared by intermediate-fusion approaches that generate virtual 3-D points by depth-completing a 2-D image: the resulting virtual point density is highest near the sensor, where real LiDAR coverage is already adequate, and falls off at longer ranges, where additional coverage is most needed. The architecture introduces two components to address this imbalance.

The first, Stochastic Voxel Discard (StVD), randomly drops a fraction of virtual voxels during training in the near-range region where real LiDAR coverage is already sufficient. This prevents the model from over-fitting to redundant near-range virtual points and forces it to develop useful representations for the sparser far-range regime.

The second, the Noise-Resistant Sub-manifold Convolution (NRConv), encodes each voxel jointly in 3-D LiDAR space and 2-D image space. Image features sampled at the projected pixel location of each voxel are concatenated before the linear encoding step. Because depth-completed virtual points can carry positional noise when the depth estimate is inaccurate, NRConv suppresses this noise by learning a confidence weighting from the local LiDAR density, giving less influence to virtual features in regions where depth estimation is unreliable. VirConvNet achieved first and second place on the KITTI 3-D car detection leaderboard at the time of publication [k].

In this project, VirConvNet is adapted from bounding-box detection to per-point semantic segmentation. The SSD detection head is replaced with a BEV segmentation head, and per-point class logits are gathered by indexing the BEV output at each point's pillar coordinate. Virtual point generation and pillarization are performed on the CPU in NumPy; an auxiliary 2-D segmentation loss applied at the image feature map maintains a gradient path to the image backbone across the NumPy computation boundary.

3.3   Overview of Path Planning Algorithms in Robotics

Path planning in robotics is the process of generating a route for a robot to move from its starting point to its destination. This route must avoid obstacles while also following additional imposed limitations. These algorithms are used before the robot starts its motion.

Path planning usually begins with an environment modeling step, which creates a map of the area where the robot operates. This is followed by a path searching step, where a feasible path that starts from the initial point and which ends at the goal is computed. Path searching is done entirely within the model of the environment, this step also being where constraints are considered. Once the path is determined, the robot can execute its movement.

Broadly, path planning algorithms can be classified into analytical, learning-based, and hybrid methods. Analytical methods, also called classical methods, are represented by traditional rule-based path planning algorithms, and can be categorized into graph-based planning algorithms and sampling-based algorithms. Learning-based methods were developed much later and are mainly inspired by innovations found in nature, with examples including evolutionary algorithms, swarm optimization, and neural network-based planning [5]. These methods utilize adaptive or population-based search techniques, which let the algorithm improve or learn solutions incrementally. They are appropriately used when dealing with complex or high-dimensional problems. Hybrid methods employ the use of elements from both analytical and learning-based methods.

3.3.1   Graph-Search Algorithms

Graph-based algorithms work by dividing the environment at predetermined points into two elements: nodes, which represent possible positions for the robot, and edges, which account for all possible transitions between nodes. The most notable graph-search algorithm is A*, widely recognized for its efficiency in optimization tasks in navigation and robotics [6], and upon which many variations have been built.

Other examples of graph-search algorithms include Dijkstra's algorithm, Breadth-First Search and Depth-First Search [7]. They can, however, suffer in robotic applications within large environments due to their lack of a heuristic function component. In path generation, the heuristic greatly reduces the number of evaluated nodes by assigning values called weights to the transitions of the graph. It is used as a cost component and is typically chosen to be proportional to the direct distance from the node to the destination. However, it can also be chosen according to implementation-specific criteria.

A* has a heuristic component. However, the algorithm is best suited for static environments where obstacles are known beforehand, much like the other graph-based methods presented above. This computational cost represents a problem for real-time, dynamic robotic applications. For this reason, A* variations with live replanning capabilities have been introduced in algorithms such as LPA* (Lifelong Planning A*), D*, and D* lite [8].

The main advantage of D* lite over A* is its ability to reuse the parts of the paths that are not affected by obstacles when one is encountered. A comparative analysis of A* and D* lite done on a differential drive robot is presented in [9], where the quality of produced trajectories and the required computational time are measured and compared. According to the results, D* lite produces shorter paths on average, and is faster to compute in dynamic environments. However, A* generally outperforms D* lite in static environments.

Graph-based path planning methods find a large range of applications in differing types of robots. In [10], an example of an implementation of a D* lite variation for a coastline and harbor unmanned surface vehicle path finding is shown, while [11] implements A* in unmanned aerial vehicle navigation.

3.3.2   Sampling-Based Algorithms

The other subset of analytical algorithms are the sampling-based algorithms. The methods employ a stochastic approach to pathfinding. Unlike the graph-based methods that require a complete environmental map, these algorithms build nodes and edges incrementally. They do this by randomly sampling points in the free space, creating nodes at these points and connecting them. The Rapidly-Exploring Random Tree (RRT) and the Probabilistic Roadmap algorithm (PRM) are two examples of this approach.

The RRT algorithm [12] works by gradually building a tree that starts at the initial position. At each step, a random point in the configuration space is chosen, along with the closest node to that point. At each step a random point in the configuration space is sampled, along with the closest node to it. New nodes are generated by expanding in the direction of the point by a fixed step size, unless an obstacle intersection is detected. The process repeats until a node is generated within close distance to the goal node, at which point those two are connected and the full path is completed.

While RRT is suited for unmapped environments, the generated paths are often jagged and can have a slow convergence to optimal results [13]. In many situations a post-processing step for path smoothing is used. RRT* [14] represents an extension of the base RRT algorithm which incorporates a rewiring step which constantly refines the structure of the tree as it is built.

The PRM [15] algorithm, employs a two-phase approach: a learning phase and a query phase. In the learning phase, random configurations are sampled from the environment, which are connected through straight lines to its neighbors using a local planner, unless the generated connections collide with obstacles. This step forms a roadmap, represented through a discrete graph with nodes and transitions. The query step connects the start and goal nodes to the roadmap, after which graph search methods such as A* or Dijkstra's are applied to find the best path.

This algorithm is quite efficient in situations where the same map is reused multiple times with differing start and goal configurations. Moreover, the performance of PRM degrades in narrow passages due to the lower probability of sampling valid, non-obstacle intersecting points [16].

3.3.3   Evolutionary Algorithms

An important learning-based pathfinding algorithm subset is represented by evolutionary algorithms. They work by using simulated evolution processes to find optimal paths. One of the most popular techniques in this category is the Genetic Algorithm (GA) [17], in which a new path is represented using a gene-containing chromosome. Genes represent the robot's intermediate positions, from the starting point to the goal. The evolutionary algorithm improves the set of potential paths through three main operations: selection, crossover, and mutation.

During the selection process, candidate paths with a better fitness score, i.e. those that are shorter, safer, or smoother, have a greater likelihood of being chosen for the production of offspring. In the crossover process, genes from two parents are recombined to produce an offspring. In the mutation process, a small amount of randomness is applied to each gene in order to ensure that the population does not converge prematurely and to retain sufficient variety.

The result of this process is that the algorithm will explore a large number of combinations of path segments simultaneously, while continuing to favor those combinations that provide better solutions. As the population continues to evolve, the paths will converge towards the optimal solution for the robot to utilize when navigating in the environment.

In [18], a GA approach is implemented and tested in a mobile robot path planning application. The environment in which the tests took place was static and a grid. This paper demonstrates that GA can evaluate multiple paths simultaneously. However, a main disadvantage noted in the study is the tendency of GA towards premature convergence in highly constrained environments. Another noted disadvantage is the high computational cost, which constrains GA implementation in real world solutions.

The GA approach can also be used for Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) planning, as shown in [19]. The paper introduces a method where candidate paths are encoded as genetic sequences and evolved to avoid local optima. Importantly, the fitness function is modified to encode the kinematic and safety constraints of UAVs. Similarly to the previous study, the high computational cost is also noted.

3.3.4   Swarm Intelligence Algorithms

Another category of learning-based methods is the Swarm Intelligence method, which is based on the behavior of biological systems such as flocks of birds or colonies of ants. Within it, the Particle Swarm Optimization [20] and the Ant Colony Optimization [21] algorithms are notable examples.

In the Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) algorithm, a set of candidate solutions called particles explore the space of solutions. Each particle maintains a memory of the best solution it has discovered so far, called the personal best, as well as the global best solution discovered so far by the entire particle swarm.

At each iteration, the velocity of each particle is updated using a weighted combination of three components: inertia, a cognitive factor, and a social factor. While the inertia factor maintains the previous velocity, the cognitive and social factors move the particle towards the best personal and global solutions, respectively. This iterative process of information sharing and the balance of exploitation and exploration allows the swarm to gradually converge towards the optimal or near-optimal solutions in the search space.

The Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) method is inspired by the paths ants take between sources of their food and their nest, and the pheromone trails generated by them. Similarly to analytical methods, the environment is represented as a graph, but this method additionally uses artificial pheromones deposited by agents while moving and exploring paths.

The solution is generated probabilistically by using the pheromone levels left on the edges and a heuristic component. Once the agents have completed a path, pheromones are deposited proportionally to the quality of the solution, and older trails are slowly evaporated over time, reducing their deposited values and encouraging exploration. The algorithm will repeatedly build solutions to the problem and update pheromone values, ultimately converging towards the optimal or near-optimal solution for the problem.

3.3.5   Neural Network-Based Planners

A category of learning-based planning algorithms which have seen a great increase in interest in recent years are the Neural Network-based planners (NN) [22], inspired by the behavior of their biological neural counterparts. They are built from interconnected nodes, called neurons, that either propagate or inhibit signals. A key advantage of NNs is their ability to learn patterns from input data, which makes them very adaptable to different situations.

Neural networks are used in many applications, such as natural language processing and image detection and generation. However, path planning implementations have specific challenges. Unlike the case of image classification or language processing, the neural network has to deal with the dynamic nature of the environment while respecting geometric and kinematic constraints. Early implementations were based on simple feedforward neural networks that mapped the input from the environment directly to the motion commands. However, these solutions were found to be deficient in terms of the ability to generalize to different environments.

Recent solutions to the problem use a fusion of different deep learning architectures such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for the feature extraction and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) for decision-making. While reinforcement learning-based solutions have been found to be particularly effective in path planning, they also have drawbacks. The sim-to-real gap is one such example, where neural networks trained on virtual environments can fail to generalize to real-world implementations. Another issue posed by this architecture is the high computational, monetary, and time costs associated with the training process. Acquiring the relevant datasets for each implementation represents another issue, as they greatly vary from implementation to implementation.

In [23], an approach combining odometry, deep learning-based artificial vision, and SLAM is presented. The system uses an NVIDIA Jetson Nano embedded system to control an autonomous mobile robot. It can recognize obstacles and convey their locations to a local navigation path planner by using two CNN architectures, ResNet18 and YOLOv3. This allows for real-time scene perception and object detection. The ResNet18-based vision system demonstrated the efficacy of deep learning algorithms in autonomous mobile robot applications, achieving an average accuracy of 98.5% across simulation and real-world trials.

3.3.6   Hybrid Pathfinding Methods

A main observation identified in the survey presented in [24] is that many state-of-the-art approaches in literature use hybrids of analytical and learning-based methods. In general, analytical path planning methods are unreliable in dynamic environment implementations, where they can be computationally expensive and thus harder to scale. Another noted observation is that the main analytical algorithm that is adapted in hybrid approaches is A*, which is usually paired with either genetic algorithms or deep learning. There are many such examples of hybrid algorithms, most of which are experimental in nature. The following paragraphs will discuss a couple of notable examples.

One example of such an algorithm is Neural A*, introduced in [25], which uses a heuristic determined using a deep learning approach that is then applied in a traditional A* algorithm for path finding. Neural A* can be applied to point-to-point graph searches, as well as top-down raw image inputs. It manages to generate a path faster by exploring significantly fewer nodes than A*. In spite of the improvements made by this approach, significant disadvantages also present in A* still remain, foremost being the fact that neural A* is still mainly suited only for static environments. According to the conclusion of the paper, the presented implementation is not made to work on non-continuous spaces or those with more than two spatial dimensions.

A solution to this problem presented in [26] is Hybrid A*, which addresses the issues posed by Neural A*. While simple A* assumes a holonomic vehicle and a discrete space, the proposed model is applied in a continuous, three-dimensional space, and considers vehicle orientation. Thus, the paths Hybrid A* generates satisfy real vehicle kinodynamic constraints. The nature of the terrain it traverses is also considered and used to optimize the path. Because of these factors, Hybrid A* can be more readily deployed in real world robotic vehicle applications.
