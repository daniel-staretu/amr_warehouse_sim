Sinopsis

Această lucrare investighează transferabilitatea arhitecturilor de percepție LiDAR–cameră din domeniul ADAS în scenarii de depozitare industrială interioară. O simulare fizică în PyBullet colectează autonom nori de puncte LiDAR și imagini RGB cu etichete semantice pentru patru clase — fundal, rafturi, lăzi și stivuitor — folosind un planificator Hybrid A*. Modelul VirConvNet (Wu et al., 2023), adaptat pentru segmentare semantică per-punct, augmentează punctele LiDAR cu trăsături de imagine și generează puncte 3D virtuale din regiunile de prim-plan ale imaginii neacoperite de LiDAR. Un studiu de ablație cuantifică contribuția individuală a fiecărui mecanism de fuziune, stabilind o linie de bază pentru percepția ADAS în medii industriale interioare.

Cuvinte cheie: LiDAR, fuziune LiDAR–cameră, segmentare semantică 3D, VirConvNet, depozitare, roboți mobili autonomi, simulare PyBullet, Hybrid A*.

Abstract

This thesis investigates the transferability of ADAS LiDAR–camera perception architectures to an indoor warehousing environment. A configurable PyBullet simulation autonomously collects synchronised LiDAR point clouds and RGB images with pixel-perfect semantic labels across four classes — background, shelves, crates, and forklifts — using a Hybrid A* navigation stack. VirConvNet (Wu et al., 2023) is adapted from multimodal bounding-box detection to per-point semantic segmentation: each LiDAR point is augmented with 32-dimensional image features from a lightweight CNN backbone, and virtual 3-D points are generated from foreground image regions not covered by LiDAR returns. A three-variant ablation isolates the contributions of image features and virtual points; a distance-zone analysis tests whether virtual augmentation specifically addresses the LiDAR density deficit at longer ranges. The work establishes a reproducible baseline for ADAS-to- warehousing perception transfer and characterises the principal indoor domain gaps.

Keywords: LiDAR, camera fusion, 3-D semantic segmentation, VirConvNet, warehousing, autonomous mobile robots, simulation, Hybrid A*.

1   INTRODUCTION

1.1   Context

Warehouse automation is accelerating rapidly, driven by sustained growth in e-commerce volumes and increasing pressure on logistics operators to reduce order fulfilment times while managing labour costs. Autonomous Mobile Robots (AMRs) and autonomous forklifts are now commercially deployed in large distribution centres, where they perform tasks such as pallet retrieval, shelf replenishment, and goods-to-person delivery. Despite their growing adoption, the perception systems on these platforms remain far less mature than those of self-driving cars. Road vehicles benefit from two decades of academic competition benchmarks — KITTI (Geiger et al., 2012), nuScenes (Caesar et al., 2020), Waymo Open Dataset (Sun et al., 2020) — and the hundreds of published architectures trained and validated on them. No equivalent public benchmark exists for indoor industrial perception.

Advanced Driver Assistance Systems (ADAS) have produced a rich body of LiDAR–camera fusion architectures optimised for road scenarios. Models such as PointPillars (Lang et al., 2019) achieve over 60 Hz inference on KITTI while matching or surpassing earlier fusion methods in 3-D bounding-box detection accuracy. VirConvNet (Wu et al., 2023) introduces virtual point generation from depth-completed image data, reaching state-of-the-art results on KITTI car detection. These architectures encode strong geometric priors learned from outdoor scenes: vehicle-sized anchor boxes, horizontal ground planes extending to tens of metres, and sensor noise profiles calibrated for outdoor atmospheric conditions.

This project asks a direct transfer question: can these ADAS-designed architectures be applied to an indoor warehousing environment — a setting with short sensor ranges, axis-aligned shelf geometry, severe object scale variation, and stable controlled lighting — with minimal modification, and if so, how much of their capability transfers and where does it break down? To answer this question reproducibly, a physics-based simulation was built to generate labelled training data, VirConvNet was retrained from scratch on that data, and its performance was characterised through quantitative evaluation and a controlled ablation study.

1.2   Problem Statement

ADAS perception models are developed and benchmarked exclusively on outdoor, road-centric datasets. No public benchmark and no evaluation baseline exists for LiDAR-based 3-D semantic perception in indoor warehousing. Three specific gaps motivate this project:

Gap 1 — Absence of a labelled indoor perception dataset. All major 3-D perception benchmarks are outdoor and vehicle-centric. Annotating a real warehouse dataset at the point-cloud level requires either manual labelling, which is prohibitively expensive at scale, or a simulation that provides ground-truth labels for free. Neither a standard simulation platform nor a standard evaluation protocol for indoor warehouse perception exists in the published literature.

Gap 2 — Unknown model transferability. It is unknown whether ADAS-designed architectures apply to warehousing without retraining or structural modification. Indoor LiDAR scans differ from outdoor scans in almost every statistical property: point density falls off more steeply with range (no distant vehicle lanes), the dominant horizontal structure is a flat floor rather than a road surface, shelf geometry produces long narrow planar return patterns that resemble no KITTI object class, and the maximum useful range is 15–20 m rather than 70–100 m. Whether the feature representations learned from road scenarios transfer to these conditions is an open empirical question.

Gap 3 — Unknown fusion benefit in stable indoor lighting. The advantage of LiDAR–camera fusion over LiDAR-only detection is well-documented in outdoor ADAS literature, where camera quality degrades at night, in rain, and in back-lit conditions. In a warehouse with stable fluorescent or LED lighting, this source of fusion benefit is absent. Whether camera information — specifically image texture and semantic segmentation masks — still provides a meaningful boost over LiDAR geometry alone in stable indoor conditions is untested.

1.3   Objectives

The project pursues five concrete objectives that together constitute a complete end-to-end pipeline from environment modelling to model evaluation:

  1. Simulation platform. Develop a configurable 28 × 30 m warehouse simulation in PyBullet with a VLP-16-inspired LiDAR sensor (360° × 16 channels, 5 760 rays/scan, 20 m maximum range) and a synchronised RGB camera (640 × 480, 90° vertical FoV), producing per-scan semantic labels for four object classes at zero annotation cost via the PyBullet built-in segmentation renderer.
  2. Autonomous navigation for data collection. Implement a Hybrid A* navigation stack — comprising the planner, a Pure Pursuit path-tracking controller, and a background replanning thread — to drive the robot autonomously through the warehouse, ensuring dense, reproducible spatial coverage during data collection without manual teleoperation.
  3. Labelled multi-modal dataset. Collect a synchronised, semantically labelled dataset of LiDAR point clouds and RGB images across four classes: background (0), shelf (1), crate (2), forklift (3). The dataset must include at least 5 000 frames with a reproducible 80/20 train/ validation split aligned across both modalities.
  4. VirConvNet adapted for warehouse semantic segmentation. Adapt VirConvNet (Wu et al., 2023) from its original multimodal bounding-box detection formulation to per-point semantic segmentation by replacing the detection head with a BEV segmentation head; train it on the collected paired LiDAR–image data; and evaluate using per-class IoU, mIoU, precision, recall, and F1-score on the held-out validation set.
  5. Ablation study and domain gap characterisation. Decompose the VirConvNet fusion gain into image-feature and virtual-point components through a three-variant controlled ablation; analyse recognition accuracy across forward-distance bins; and characterise the principal domain gap factors between the outdoor ADAS literature and the indoor warehousing setting.

1.4   Structure of the Thesis

The remainder of the thesis is organised as follows.

Chapter 2 (Requirements Analysis) establishes the scientific motivation in detail, defines the three research gaps, and delimits the scope of the project by identifying what is and is not addressed.

Chapter 3 (State of the Art) reviews the eleven papers in the documentation folder across six areas: LiDAR-only 3-D detection (point-based, voxel-based, BEV-based, and pillar-based methods), camera-only 3-D detection, LiDAR–camera fusion paradigms and ExistenceMap-PointPillars, improved PointPillars variants, path planning with Hybrid A*, and the relationship between SLAM and this project. The review contextualises the two model choices and positions the project within the published literature.

Chapter 4 (Proposed Solution) presents the overall system architecture across its four subsystems — simulation, perception, navigation, and training pipeline — and documents the design decisions and trade-offs that shaped the implementation.

Chapter 5 (Implementation Details) describes the algorithms and data structures implemented in each source module, covering the simulation world construction, the LiDAR sensor and camera pipeline, the Hybrid A* planner and path-tracking controller, and the VirConvNet architecture as adapted for warehouse semantic segmentation.

Chapter 6 (Evaluation of Results) presents and analyses the VirConvNet training and validation results, including the three-variant ablation study, the distance-zone breakdown, and the reflection on what the quantitative findings imply for the central research question.

Chapter 7 (Conclusions) summarises the contributions, states the research conclusions, acknowledges the limitations of the current work, and identifies directions for future work including real hardware deployment and SLAM integration.

2   REQUIREMENTS ANALYSIS

2.1   Scientific Motivation

Autonomous mobile robots and autonomous forklifts deployed in industrial warehouses must perceive and classify two distinct categories of objects in their environment: static infrastructure — shelves, walls, loading bays — and dynamic obstacles — crates, pallets, and other vehicles or workers. LiDAR is the preferred sensor for this role for three reasons. First, it provides metric depth measurements directly, without the ill-posed monocular depth estimation problem that limits camera-only approaches. Second, it is invariant to warehouse lighting conditions: fluorescent flicker, shadows cast by tall shelving, and back-lighting near loading doors do not affect the ranging accuracy of a time-of-flight scanner. Third, the per-point intensity channel provides a weak but direct material reflectivity estimate, which is useful for distinguishing metallic shelf surfaces from the painted concrete floor.

Despite these advantages, the entire 3-D LiDAR perception literature is built around outdoor, road-centric benchmarks. Indoor industrial scenarios introduce a distinct set of challenges not addressed by existing models:

  • Short sensor range. In a 28 × 30 m warehouse the effective LiDAR range is 15–20 m, compared to 70–100 m for highway ADAS. Point density therefore falls off over a compressed distance scale; objects at 15 m receive the same return density as objects at 70 m do in outdoor KITTI scans.
  • Near-uniform horizontal floor plane. The warehouse floor is a large flat background surface that contributes the majority of LiDAR returns and dominates the feature space. Outdoor scans are dominated by a smaller road surface bounded by kerbs and vegetation, which provides natural segmentation cues absent indoors.
  • Extreme object scale variation. Shelf units in this simulation span 4 m × 12 m × 2.8 m (width × length × height), roughly 20× the volume of a typical KITTI car. Crates are 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 m, smaller than a KITTI pedestrian. No single anchor box resolution simultaneously resolves both extremes well.
  • Stable, controlled lighting. Warehouses operate under uniform artificial illumination. The primary motivation for LiDAR–camera fusion in outdoor ADAS — degraded camera quality at night, in rain, or in fog — is therefore absent. Whether fusion still helps in a well-lit indoor environment is a non-trivial question.
  • No weather-induced sensor degradation. Outdoor LiDAR scans exhibit spurious returns from rain droplets and signal attenuation in fog. Indoor scans have none of these artefacts; the dominant noise sources are quantisation, surface reflectivity variation, and multi-path reflections from smooth metallic surfaces.

Three explicit research gaps follow directly from the mismatch between the ADAS literature and warehousing requirements:

Gap 1 — Dataset. No public benchmark for LiDAR-based 3-D semantic perception in indoor warehousing exists. All major benchmarks (KITTI, nuScenes, Waymo, ScanNet) are either outdoor and vehicle- centric or indoor and RGB-D-only. Without a labelled warehouse dataset it is impossible to train or evaluate any supervised learning approach for this domain. This project addresses Gap 1 by constructing a simulation capable of generating arbitrarily large amounts of labelled data.

Gap 2 — Model transferability. It is unknown whether pillar-based and virtual-point-based architectures designed for road scenarios can be applied to warehousing with minimal modification. VirConvNet builds on the PointPillars pillar encoding — pillar-level geometry discretised over a large BEV grid, feature representations learned from car and pedestrian geometry — which encodes strong outdoor-scene priors that may or may not be compatible with the different scale and structure of warehouse objects. Answering Gap 2 requires retraining the architecture on warehouse data and measuring whether the resulting model achieves meaningful accuracy across all four object classes.

Gap 3 — Fusion benefit in stable indoor lighting. The advantage of LiDAR–camera fusion measured on outdoor benchmarks may not transfer to warehousing, where the principal justification for including camera data — robustness to lighting variation — does not apply. VirConvNet's virtual point mechanism is specifically designed to fill in LiDAR coverage gaps using camera-derived depth estimates; whether this mechanism still yields a measurable improvement when the warehouse camera produces reliable, stable images throughout the operating cycle is the core empirical question addressed by the ablation in Chapter 6.

2.2   Research Scope

The research scope is defined by five in-scope items and four explicit exclusions.

In scope:

  1. A configurable physics-based simulation of an indoor warehouse in PyBullet, including a VLP-16-inspired LiDAR sensor, a forward-facing RGB camera, and semantic label generation for four object classes.
  2. A fully autonomous data collection pipeline driven by Hybrid A* navigation, producing synchronised, labelled LiDAR–image pairs.
  3. Adaptation, training, and evaluation of VirConvNet for multimodal per-point semantic segmentation on the collected warehouse dataset.
  4. A three-variant ablation study decomposing the VirConvNet fusion contribution (LiDAR-only vs. LiDAR + image features vs. full VirConvNet with virtual points) and a distance-zone analysis characterising range-dependent accuracy.
  5. A quantitative characterisation of the domain gap between outdoor ADAS perception and indoor warehousing, providing a reproducible baseline for future work.

Out of scope:

  1. Real hardware deployment. All experiments are conducted entirely within the PyBullet simulation. Mounting sensors on a physical forklift or AMR and validating models on real warehouse data is identified as the most important direction for future work (Section 7.4) but is not pursued in this thesis.
  2. Multi-robot and dynamic agent scenarios. The simulation contains one robot and one stationary forklift. No multi-agent interactions, moving obstacles, or pedestrian traffic are modelled. Dynamic object diversity in the training data is limited to the fixed-position crate and forklift instances.
  3. Simultaneous Localisation and Mapping (SLAM). The simulation provides the robot's position and orientation directly from the PyBullet rigid-body state, effectively bypassing the SLAM problem. In a real-world deployment, a LiDAR-SLAM front-end (e.g., LIO-SAM, Cartographer) would be a prerequisite to the perception layer developed here, but it is not implemented in this project. The sensor data formats (point clouds in sensor frame, per-point labels, BEV representations) are directly compatible with standard SLAM pipelines, leaving the integration as future work.
  4. 3-D object detection and bounding-box regression. VirConvNet is evaluated as a semantic segmentation system (per-point class labels). Bounding-box regression, instance segmentation, and object tracking are not evaluated. The choice of segmentation over detection reflects the availability of per-point labels from the simulation renderer and the desire to evaluate all four classes — including shelves, whose large irregular geometry makes anchor-based detection non-trivial — within a single unified metric (mIoU).
